The action is built to Steinway's published regulation distances for the Model D and to lever lengths measured by piano technicians, listed in the table below. Part outlines, the key length and the pedal linkage under the keybed are simplified and are not Steinway drawings. The action plays about 30 times slower than a real key stroke, and string movement is exaggerated so it can be seen.

What happens when a key goes down

  1. The key is a lever. Its front goes down 10.2 mm and its back end rises about half as far.
  2. The capstan on the key lifts the wippen, which carries the jack.
  3. The jack pushes the knuckle under the hammer shank, and the hammer swings up toward the string, 47.6 mm above its rest position.
  4. Just before the string, the jack's toe meets the let-off button. The jack slips out from under the knuckle, so the hammer flies the last 1.6 mm freely.
  5. The hammer strikes the string and bounces back at once, leaving the string free to ring. Meanwhile the back of the key has lifted the damper off the string.
  6. The backcheck catches the falling hammer. The repetition lever holds it part-way up, so the jack can slide back under and the note can be repeated quickly.
  7. When the key is released, everything returns and the damper drops onto the string and stops the sound.
