The piano is modelled by hand to the real overall size of a concert grand, with 88 keys and hammers, 236 strings, and the standard double-escapement grand action. Part shapes and positions are simplified for teaching and are not a maker's drawings. The action plays about 30 times slower than a real key stroke, and string movement is exaggerated so it can be seen.

What happens when a key goes down

  1. The key is a lever. Its front goes down about 10 mm and its back end rises.
  2. The capstan on the key lifts the wippen, which carries the jack.
  3. The jack pushes the knuckle under the hammer shank, and the hammer swings up about 45 mm.
  4. Just before the string, the jack's toe meets the let-off button. The jack slips out from under the knuckle, so the hammer flies the last 2 mm freely.
  5. The hammer strikes the string and bounces back at once, leaving the string free to ring. Meanwhile the back of the key has lifted the damper off the string.
  6. The backcheck catches the falling hammer. The repetition lever holds it part-way up, so the jack can slide back under and the note can be repeated quickly.
  7. When the key is released, everything returns and the damper drops onto the string and stops the sound.
